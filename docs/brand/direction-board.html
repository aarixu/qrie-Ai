Why this one

  • It is ownable. Every Indian edtech app is blue or purple, copied from Western SaaS. Marigold and indigo are read instantly as Indian by the parent paying, without a single paisley.
  • It keeps work you already paid for. The amber in theme.css was contrast-tested at 9:1 with dark text; the indigo is your existing logo. Nothing is discarded.
  • It survives cheap screens. Saturated warm hues hold up on low-grade LCD panels, where dark greys band and neons blow out.
  • Warmth reads as trustworthy to the buyer, which for Class 6–12 in India is almost always a parent.

The risk

  • Warmth can read young. A Class 11 student must not feel handed a toy. The type has to carry the seriousness the colour will not — tight, confident grotesque, never rounded.
  • Marigold is close to the festival palette everything in India uses in October. Restraint on how much of it appears at once is what keeps it a brand rather than a season.

Why it could win

  • It matches when the product is used. Evening study, low light, one hand on the phone.
  • Lime on slate is genuinely unused in Indian K–12. It would be the most eye-catching thing in the Play Store category by a distance.
  • It does not look like school, which is the single loudest thing a fifteen-year-old cares about.

The risk — and it is real

  • Your target phones are mostly LCD, not OLED. So there is no battery saving, and near-black greys band and go muddy on cheap panels. The main technical argument for dark-first does not apply to your hardware.
  • It fights your existing artwork. Every generated diagram and every Wikimedia Commons image was fetched for a light board. Going dark means re-verifying the whole picture ladder.
  • Parents are the buyers, and a black app with acid green is not what reassures a parent deciding on a subscription.

Why it could win

  • Flat colour is nearly free to render. No gradients, no shadows — tiny SVGs, small downloads, fast paint on a slow phone. Colour choice is a performance decision here, not only an aesthetic one.
  • It rescues machine-made artwork. Your diagrams are planned and drawn by a model. In a riso system, flat and slightly crude reads as style rather than as a limitation.
  • Nobody in the category is near it.

The risk

  • Fluorescents fail contrast fast. Pink on newsprint will not clear 4.5:1 for body text, so the palette has to be policed constantly — the opposite of the amber decision you already made carefully.
  • It reads as a zine, not as a tutor. Charming to a designer, harder to justify to a parent paying every month.

Why it could win

  • Maximum legibility on a small screen. Thick outlines and hard edges survive a 5-inch display and a cracked screen protector better than anything else here.
  • It hides weak artwork the same way riso does — an outline system makes everything inside the outline look intentional.
  • It is the cheapest to build. No shadows to tune, no gradients to test, two border widths and you have a design system.

The risk

  • It reads as a toy to the person paying. Of the four, this is the hardest to put in front of a parent or a school principal.
  • The trend has peaked. It is already dated in 2026, and a brand you hope to run for a decade should not be timestamped this precisely.

Where I land

01, Marigold & Indigo — with the confidence of 04 borrowed for the mark and the chips, and none of its outlines in the lesson itself.

It is the only one of the four that is ownable rather than borrowed from a trend, it is the only one that keeps the contrast work already done in theme.css, and it is the only one that makes a parent and a fifteen-year-old both comfortable — which matters because one of them chooses and the other uses.

02 is the most exciting and the most wrong for your hardware. 03 and 04 are both real options if you want to be loud, and both cost you the accessibility discipline you have already built.
